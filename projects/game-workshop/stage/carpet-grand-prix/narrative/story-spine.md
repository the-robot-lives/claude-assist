# Carpet Grand Prix — Story Spine

Wordless. Nine vignettes, 8–14 seconds each, rendered in the same layered-diorama
style as gameplay — no cutaway to a different visual language, no zoom to
photorealism. One vignette per room (7) plus two at the end. No dialogue, no
on-screen text, no narrator, at any point, for any reason.

## Tone — 7-Axis Spectrum

| Axis | Position |
|---|---|
| Serious ←→ Playful | **Playful**, 80% |
| Grounded ←→ Fantastical | **Grounded**, 70% — the toys move, nothing else breaks |
| Warm ←→ Cold | **Warm**, 85% |
| Simple ←→ Complex | **Simple**, 90% |
| Hopeful ←→ Melancholy | **Melancholy**, 60% — a gentle ache under the fun |
| Intimate ←→ Epic | **Intimate**, 95% — the whole world is one house |
| Sincere ←→ Ironic | **Sincere**, 100% |

Every vignette is built to sit at this exact position. A vignette that plays broader
than 80% playful reads as slapstick and breaks the melancholy; a vignette that dips
below 60% melancholy reads as a joke with no floor under it. The two failure modes
to guard against in animation review are **cute-cruel** (a beat that is sad enough to
need an adult in the room to explain it to a five-year-old) and **cute-hollow** (a beat
so safe it could be a toy commercial). Every vignette in this document is written to
land between those two rails.

## The Nine Vignettes

The GDD's eight-point spine maps to nine unlock moments because the eighth beat,
**Resolution**, is one continuous idea told in two parts across two unlocks — the box
reopening, and what comes out of it. Everything else is one beat, one room, one
vignette.

| # | Unlocks at | Spine beat | Length |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Game start (before Playroom's first run) | 1. Equilibrium | 12s |
| 2 | Kitchen (4 medals) | 2. Inciting Incident | 10s |
| 3 | Hallway (10 medals) | 3. First Complication | 14s |
| 4 | Attic (18 medals) | 4. Rising Action | 13s |
| 5 | Garage (26 medals) | 5. Midpoint Reversal | 11s |
| 6 | Basement (34 medals) | 6. Crisis | 10s |
| 7 | Garden (44 medals) | 7. Climax | 14s |
| 8 | The Box (all 38 golds, or 60 medals total) | 8a. Resolution — the box | 9s |
| 9 | Completing the game's final gold after The Box unlocks | 8b. Resolution — the track | 12s |

### Vignette 1 — Equilibrium (12s)

*Dusk. A kid's hand pushes a red car down an orange track from the couch to the rug.*

1. **(0–3s)** Wide shot, floor level. Warm window light low and long across the
   carpet. A kid's hand — we never see above the wrist — sets a red die-cast car on
   an orange track section propped against a couch cushion.
2. **(3–6s)** The hand releases the car. It rolls down the cushion-ramp, off the end,
   and onto the rug track below. Handheld-feeling camera drift follows it, low and
   close, the way a kid's eye follows their own toy.
3. **(6–9s)** The car reaches the rug's edge, spins out gently, stops. Off-screen,
   a single beat of a kid's laugh — not a line, not a word, just breath and delight,
   mixed low, more felt than heard.
4. **(9–12s)** The hand reaches back in, doesn't pick the car up — just nudges it
   upright — and withdraws for the last time. Hold on the car, motionless, as the
   window light dims one stop. Cut to black on the held frame, not on motion.

This is the only vignette in which a human hand appears on screen. Every later
vignette is cars only, or a body glimpsed from the knees down at most — the
Midpoint Reversal is the sole exception, and even there the face is never shown.

### Vignette 2 — Inciting Incident (10s)

*Morning. A cardboard box lands on the playroom floor. Marker on the side: DONATE.
A parent's hands begin sweeping track pieces into it.*

1. **(0–2.5s)** Cold, flat morning light — a different light signature entirely from
   Vignette 1's dusk, established instantly by the room's grader so the player feels
   the time jump before anything happens. A cardboard box drops into frame, corner
   first, and settles on the rug where the car stopped last night.
2. **(2.5–5s)** Slow push-in on the box's side. A marker squeaks — audio only, no
   hand visible drawing it — and letters appear stroke by stroke: **DONATE**. This is
   the one and only place text ever appears in the entire narrative, and it is
   diegetic — words written by a character inside the fiction, not a UI overlay, not
   narration. It is never legible as anything but a handwritten word on a box.
3. **(5–8s)** A pair of adult hands, seen only from mid-forearm down, sweep across
   the rug in one unhurried motion. Three sections of orange track slide and clatter
   into the box.
4. **(8–10s)** Hold on the cars — still where the kid left them, out of frame of the
   sweep — motionless, facing the box. No car moves. Nothing has become animate yet.
   This vignette establishes the wound before the story answers it.

### Vignette 3 — First Complication (14s)

*Night. The cars move on their own for the first time — badly, wobbling, relearning.
Half the track is already in the box. They race on what's left.*

1. **(0–3s)** Full dark, house-quiet. A single wheel on Red 7 twitches. Holds. Twitches
   again. This is filmed exactly like a stop-motion test — no smoothing, the twitch
   reads as effortful, not magical.
2. **(3–6s)** Red 7 rolls two inches, overcorrects, clips a table leg, rights itself.
   Nothing about this motion is graceful — it is the vignette's whole point that they
   are bad at this, on night one, in a way the player's own bronze-medal fumbling
   run through Kitchen an hour ago has already taught them to recognise.
3. **(6–10s)** Cut wide: the surviving half-track, laid crooked across the remaining
   rug space where the donation sweep didn't reach. Three more cars wake in
   sequence — Chrome overshoots a curve and topples, The Loaf doesn't so much
   corner as plow through, Wasp alone threads it clean on the first try.
4. **(10–14s)** All four settle into a ragged, uneven line and creep forward together
   down the remaining track, engines-that-aren't-engines humming faintly higher in
   pitch than gameplay audio ever gets — this is the sound design's one moment of
   pure vulnerability before the cars find their voice.

---

### Vignette 4 — Rising Action (13s)

*The cars start stealing back track pieces, one at a time, dragging them room to room.
The house becomes a circuit.*

1. **(0–3s)** The Loaf, alone, noses a single straight section of track out from under
   the donation box's flap. It takes the full three seconds — this is heavy,
   deliberate, unglamorous labour, matching The Loaf's gameplay identity of mass over
   finesse.
2. **(3–6s)** Time-lapse-feeling montage cut: Sparks dragging a curve section across
   linoleum, sparks (the visual kind) skittering off the floor at every joint;
   Rustbucket hauling a ramp piece up over a door threshold one wheel-width at a
   time.
3. **(6–10s)** Wide establishing shot from a high corner of the hallway: track now
   runs continuously from the playroom rug, under a doorway, and partway into the
   hall — the first shot in the game where the house itself, not just a room,
   is legible as the circuit.
4. **(10–13s)** Bit — smallest of the eight — squeezes a strip of track through a gap
   under a door that none of the larger cars could fit through, and the whole line
   of waiting cars behind the door surges forward through the space Bit just opened.
   This is Bit's thesis statement before the player has met Bit in a course.

### Vignette 5 — Midpoint Reversal (11s)

*The kid comes downstairs at 2 a.m. for water and sees the track running through the
kitchen. Long pause. They step over it and go back to bed. They never mention it.*

1. **(0–2s)** Stairs, seen from the bottom, dark except for a nightlight glow. Bare
   feet — the kid, older now than in Vignette 1, though we never confirm by how
   much — descend into frame.
2. **(2–5s)** The feet stop, mid-stair, at the sight of orange track running across
   the kitchen tile below, cars frozen mid-line the instant the stairs creaked. Hold
   the stillness for a full beat longer than feels comfortable — this is the single
   longest static hold in the whole game.
3. **(5–8s)** The feet resume, step deliberately over the track rather than through
   it — the game's clearest visual statement that the kid *sees* and *chooses* — cross
   the kitchen tile, and continue toward the sink, off-frame.
4. **(8–11s)** Hold on the track and the frozen cars alone in the kitchen light for
   the last three seconds, empty of any human presence, silent except for the tap
   running once, briefly, off-screen, then stopping. The cars do not move again
   until the shot cuts to black.

This is the emotional keystone of the whole narrative arc and the only vignette
that risks showing more of the kid's body than hands or feet — deliberately still
staying beneath the shoulders. The player should not be certain, watching it, whether
what they just saw was permission or a secret being kept. Both readings are correct
and neither is confirmed later.

### Vignette 6 — Crisis (10s)

*The box is taped shut and carried to the garage. The remaining cars can only reach
the basement — the darkest, steepest, most dangerous run in the house.*

1. **(0–3s)** Adult hands, same forearm-only framing as Vignette 2, pull tape across
   the donation box's top flaps in three decisive strips. The sound design gives this
   more weight than the visual — the tape shriek is the loudest single sound effect
   in the narrative sequence, on purpose.
2. **(3–6s)** The box, now sealed, is lifted and carried away from camera, into a
   hallway, toward a door that opens onto colder, flatter garage light. It does not
   come back into frame.
3. **(6–8s)** Cut to the remaining cars gathered at the top of a basement stairwell,
   track laid down the first two steps and then stopping short over a drop into
   darkness — headlight-cone lighting only, matching the Basement room's actual
   in-game lighting rule.
4. **(8–10s)** Sparks — the recklessness-coded car — noses forward first, alone, into
   the dark stairwell ahead of the others. Cut before it reaches the bottom.

### Vignette 7 — Climax (14s)

*The Garden run. The full team, on the last unbroken stretch of track, out the back
door and down the garden path in the rain, chasing the car that got boxed.*

1. **(0–3s)** All eight cars — the full roster, together on screen for the only time
   in the narrative — cross the threshold of an open back door into rain. Wet-grass
   ambience and the sound of rain on plastic track begin immediately, sourced from
   the real Garden room audio bed, not a bespoke narrative mix.
2. **(3–6s)** Wide tracking shot down the garden path, track glistening under dusk
   rain-light, all eight in a loose, chaotic pack rather than a disciplined line —
   this is the one vignette that is allowed to look genuinely fast and joyful without
   an undertow, because it is chasing something, not fleeing or grieving.
3. **(6–10s)** What they're chasing becomes clear: a ninth silhouette, up ahead on
   the path, moving at the very edge of visibility in the rain — same proportions as
   a die-cast car but rendered as a near-black outline with no paint detail, no
   livery. This is the player's only glimpse of The Boxed One before it is playable.
4. **(10–14s)** The pack closes the distance as the path runs out at a low garden
   wall — the literal edge of the playable world. The silhouette either goes over the
   wall or is caught at its base; the frame cuts on the moment of contact, not the
   result. Rain continues over black for one full second before cut.

---

### Vignette 8 — Resolution, Part One: The Box (9s)

*The box is open on the patio. Nothing was thrown away.*

1. **(0–3s)** Static shot, flat daylight — the first true daylight scene since
   Vignette 1's dusk, and the first ever full-color, non-golden-hour lighting in the
   narrative sequence. The donate box sits on a sunlit patio slab, flaps open,
   undisturbed for what the light and the box's faded marker ink suggest has been a
   long time.
2. **(3–6s)** Slow push-in on the box's interior: every piece of track that was ever
   swept away in Vignette 2 is still there, dusty but intact, along with cars that
   never got the chance to wake — the six purely cosmetic liveries, glimpsed here for
   the only time as a group.
3. **(6–9s)** Hold on the box, empty of any figure, in full silence except ambient
   birdsong. No car moves in this vignette. It is a still life, not a scene — the
   calm after Vignette 7's motion is the entire point.

---

### Vignette 9 — Resolution, Part Two: The Track (12s)

*The kid — older now, taller — is laying new track across the grass. Not for
themselves. For someone smaller, standing behind them.*

1. **(0–3s)** Legs and forearms only, as established convention throughout — but
   visibly longer-limbed, taller, than every prior appearance. The figure kneels on
   the lawn and unspools a length of orange track from a fresh, unopened set, not
   from the donate box.
2. **(3–6s)** A second, smaller pair of feet enters frame behind the kneeling figure
   — bare, hesitant, clearly younger. The taller figure's hand reaches back without
   looking and guides the smaller figure forward by the shoulder, off-frame above.
3. **(6–9s)** The track is laid in a simple loop on the grass — deliberately smaller
   and simpler than any course the player has raced, a Playroom-tier layout, closing
   the shape of the whole game.
4. **(9–12s)** Both figures step back out of frame. Hold on the empty loop of track
   in the grass, in the same warm late light as Vignette 1's opening dusk — the only
   two vignettes in the game to share that exact light signature — until, after a
   beat, Red 7 rolls into frame on its own and takes the first lap. Cut to black on
   motion, mirroring Vignette 1's cut to black on stillness. The story ends where it
   began, inverted.

---

## The Rule

There is no dialogue, no on-screen text (beyond the single diegetic "DONATE" marker
in Vignette 2), and no narrator anywhere in Carpet Grand Prix — not in the vignettes,
not in menus, not in tutorial framing. Every beat above is designed to be fully
legible from staging, motion, light, and sound alone. If a vignette draft requires a
caption to be understood, the vignette is wrong, not the rule.
