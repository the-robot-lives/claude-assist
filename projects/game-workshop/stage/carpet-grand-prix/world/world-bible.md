# Carpet Grand Prix — World Bible

The entire world of Carpet Grand Prix is one suburban house. There is no larger
setting, no other house, no outside world beyond a garden and a low wall at its
edge. This document is the set of hard rules that keep that world consistent across
38 courses, 9 vignettes, and every future piece of level or narrative content.

## The house as the whole world

The house is not a hub connecting to other worlds — it *is* the world. Seven rooms
(Playroom, Kitchen, Hallway, Attic, Garage, Basement, Garden) make up the entirety
of the playable and narrative space. Nothing exists outside them except the low
garden wall at the very edge of the Garden room, which functions as the literal edge
of the fiction — the point past which the game does not extend, narratively or
mechanically, ever.

This scale constraint is deliberate and load-bearing: Intimate/Epic sits at 95%
Intimate on the tone spectrum specifically because the entire emotional and physical
scope of the story never leaves a floor plan a real eight-year-old could walk in
under a minute. Nothing in this game should ever imply there is a second house, a
neighbourhood, or a world outside the windows worth naming.

## Rules of the fiction

These four rules are absolute and none of them is ever explained on-screen, in a
vignette, in UI text, or in any accessibility description. They are simply always
true, and the player is trusted to infer them from repetition across nine vignettes
and thirty-eight courses.

1. **The cars move only at night.** Every vignette in which a car moves under its own
   power is lit with night-interior or night-exterior lighting. Daylight vignettes
   (Vignette 1's dusk before full dark, Vignette 8's full daylight) never show
   independent car motion — Vignette 1 ends on stillness before dark falls, and
   Vignette 8 is a still life specifically because it is daytime.
2. **The cars move only when unobserved.** Nothing in the fiction ever shows a car
   moving while a human is looking directly at it. The Midpoint Reversal vignette is
   the rule's clearest illustration, not an exception to it — the moment the stairs
   creak, the cars are already still, and they do not resume until the kid's feet
   and the tap have both gone quiet and left the frame. The game never depicts the
   mechanism by which a car "knows" it's observed. It simply is always already still
   by the time anyone would see otherwise, and always already moving again by the
   time the frame returns to them alone.
3. **Nothing else in the house is animate.** Furniture does not shift, doors do not
   open themselves, no other toy in the house moves independently, and no other
   object develops agency at any point across the whole game. The magic — such as it
   is — belongs to the die-cast cars alone. This is a hard boundary specifically to
   protect the Grounded/Fantastical axis sitting at 70% Grounded: one impossible
   thing is a fable; a house full of impossible things is a different, broader
   genre this game is not trying to be.
4. **No magic is ever explained.** There is no origin scene, no lore fragment, no
   accessibility text, and no developer commentary anywhere in the shipped product
   that accounts for why the cars move. The story spine treats it exactly the way a
   child treats their own toys coming alive in imaginative play: as simply, flatly
   true, requiring no more justification than gravity.

## Scale — the house, enormous

The game renders everything at approximately 1:64 scale, matching real die-cast toy
car proportions, and the diorama camera and parallax system (see the flesh GDD's
tilt-viewpoint mechanic) is calibrated so that ordinary house furniture reads as
vast at that scale: a couch cushion is a cliff face, a kitchen tile grout line is a
canyon a car can be lost in, a door threshold is a ramp requiring real commitment of
speed to clear. Every room's course design leans into this intentionally — hazards
are never abstract game-geometry, they are always a real household object or
surface behaving the way that object would behave to something the size of a
Hot Wheels car: a spilled-juice patch is sticky the way juice actually is, an open
rug seam is a genuine drop-off, a rafter gap in the Attic is exactly the width of a
real roof joist.

## The track as a scarce resource

Track is never a limitless or renewable resource in the fiction. There is exactly
one physical set of orange plastic track in this story, and every piece of it is
accounted for across the narrative: swept into the donation box in the Inciting
Incident, then physically dragged back out and reassembled by the cars themselves,
piece by piece, room by room, across the Rising Action. This is why course
unlocks are gated by medals rather than currency or time — mechanically that gate
exists for pacing, but narratively it maps onto the idea that every course the
player races on is track the cars have personally reclaimed, one stolen section at
a time, before the player was ever handed the wheel. By the time the player reaches
the Garden, every foot of track underneath every car has a specific, implied history
of having been fought for.

The corollary rule, upheld strictly: track pieces are never duplicated, generated,
or magically produced anywhere in the story. The Attic, Garage, Basement, and Garden
rooms all reuse and extend the same finite pool established as "what didn't get
swept into the box" in Vignette 2 and "what got dragged back" across Vignette 4 —
which is also why the Crisis vignette, in which the box is taped shut and hauled
away with whatever track never got reclaimed still inside it, reads as a real and
permanent loss rather than a temporary setback. What's in the box when it's sealed
does not come back until Resolution, and what comes back then is treated as found,
not recreated.
