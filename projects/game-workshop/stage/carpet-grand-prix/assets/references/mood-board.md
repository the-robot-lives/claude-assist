# Carpet Grand Prix — Mood Board Reference

Reference points for building this game's visual and material identity. No image
files — this document describes exactly what to look at and exactly what to take
from it, so a reference photo, a real object, or a memory can all stand in for the
same direction.

## Hot Wheels and die-cast track systems

**Look at:** an actual orange plastic Hot Wheels track set — the connector clips,
the way sections bow slightly under a car's weight, the stress-whitening plastic at
every joint after repeated use, the way a loop or ramp section is moulded in one
piece with visible sprue-gate nubs never fully sanded off.

**Take:** the specific saturated safety-orange of the track plastic as the game's
track-colour anchor across every room (the per-room palette table varies the *rail*
colour room to room, but the base track orange stays close to this real-world
reference throughout). Take the stress-whitening at the joints as the texture detail
that should read in the procedurally generated track surface — a brand-new,
pristine track reads as wrong for this story; the track has been raced on for years.

## Die-cast casting seams and base plates

**Look at:** a real die-cast toy car turned upside down — the flat stamped-metal
base plate, the screw or rivet holding the shell to the chassis, the visible parting
line where the two halves of the casting mould met, running around the car's
midline.

**Take:** the seam line and base plate as the literal visual anchor for the "die-cast,
not photoreal" pillar. Every car in the roster should show this seam somewhere in
its top-down silhouette, rendered as a thin, consistent line rather than sculpted
detail — it is meant to read instantly as "this is a toy," not as a scuff or a
shadow. The Boxed One's design (no livery, no seams visible) is a deliberate
violation of this reference, and that violation is the whole point of its design —
see `narrative/characters/the-boxed-one.md`.

## 1970s–80s suburban interiors

**Look at:** real estate and family-photo-archive reference from the period — shag
carpet in blue-grey and avocado tones, patterned vinyl kitchen flooring, runner rugs
over hardwood in hallways, unfinished plywood attics with a single pull-chain bulb,
wood-panelled or bare-concrete garages, unfinished basements with exposed
utility fixtures.

**Take:** the specific material and colour palette of that period as the baseline
for every room's floor and light source (see `assets/art-direction.md`'s per-room
table) — this game is not set in any specific decade on purpose, but the material
vocabulary of that era (shag, vinyl tile, runner rugs, bare plywood, pull-chain
bulbs) reads as timelessly "a house," rather than pinning the fiction to a moment
a player might date-stamp and then find dated. Avoid anything that reads as
distinctly contemporary (recessed LED lighting, engineered hardwood, open-plan
kitchen islands) — the house should feel like it could be any decade from the 1960s
to the 1990s, never later.

## Night-lit rooms

**Look at:** real interior photography of rooms lit by a single practical source
after dark — a hallway lit only by a door gap, a kitchen lit by one fluorescent tube
left on overnight, a garage lit by a buzzing strip light, a basement with no
window at all, lit only by whatever a flashlight or headlight throws.

**Take:** the specific quality of *single-source* lighting — one light, one clear
direction, one consistent shadow angle per room — as the hard constraint behind the
"light comes from the room, not the game" pillar. No room should ever show two
light sources competing for shadow direction; the moment a scene needs to be
legible from a second angle, that legibility has to come from the height-as-colour
system, not from adding a second light.

## Toy-scale hazard reference

**Look at:** a die-cast car's-eye view of an actual household surface — a spilled
drop of juice on tile seen at eye level with a toy car, a rug's pile seen close
enough that individual fibres read as terrain, a doorway threshold seen as a ramp
rather than a strip of moulding, an oil stain on a garage floor seen as a distinct
dark pool rather than a faint discoloration.

**Take:** the principle that every in-game hazard should be recognisably a real
household substance or surface behaving exactly the way it would to something the
size of a toy car — never an invented game-only hazard shape. This reference
category exists to keep the art and level-design departments honest against the
world bible's rule that the house's scale, however vast it reads at 1:64, is never
allowed to become abstract or fantastical.
